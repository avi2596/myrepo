Every month is not created equal

Twenty-five years of S&P 500 monthly returns plus the year to date, laid out year by year. Average a month across a quarter century and a seasonal shape appears that no single year shows on its own. Every month here counts toward that average — including the one still running, carried at its month-to-date return.

What the grid says

November is the standout

Best month on every measure: +2.53% average, +2.80% median, and up in 77% of its 25 observations — the highest hit rate on the board. Strip out its best year (2020, +10.75%) and it still averages +2.18%, so this is not one outlier carrying a month.

September is the weakest, but not the way it looks

At −1.15% it has the lowest average of the twelve, down in 12 of 25 years. Its median, though, is +0.43%: the typical September is mildly positive and the average is dragged under by a handful of routs — 2002 −11.0%, 2022 −9.3%, 2008 −9.1%.

February is the genuinely negative one

Four months carry a negative average — February, June, August, September — but February alone is also negative at the median (−0.18%), and it wins least often of any month (50%). Where September's weakness is a few disasters, February's is the ordinary case.

Mean and median disagree, and that is the finding

January averages a flat +0.17% but has a +1.39% median — 2009 (−8.6%) and 2008 (−6.1%) do the damage. October is the mirror image: a +2.04% median against a +1.42% average, because of a single −16.9% in 2008, the worst cell in the grid. Read both rows.

One new observation moves a month

April 2026 came in at +10.42%, the second-best April in the sample. That one month lifted April's average from +1.74% to +2.08% and made it the second-best month of the year — an observation landing 8.7 points above the old mean moves it by 8.7/26, a third of a point. At this sample size every new month has real leverage, which is the cost of keeping the averages current.

The winter half really does out-earn the summer half

November through April averages +5.41% in total; May through October averages +2.51%. The old "sell in May" split is visible in this sample — but note the summer half is still positive, so the seasonal edge is in the rate of gain, not in a loss to avoid.

Volatility is seasonal too

September and October carry the widest spreads (standard deviation 4.98 and 5.72 against a low of 3.45 in August). Autumn is not merely weaker on average, it is the least predictable stretch of the year.

Any single year ignores all of this

Scan any row across and the seasonal pattern is invisible; 2022 posted its best month in July and its worst in April. The pattern only exists in aggregate, across a sample small enough that a couple of crises can move a month's average by a full percentage point.
